Trust and safety aren’t reestablished through grand gestures or the right words — they’re rebuilt through consistent, boring reliability over time.

Here’s what actually matters:

**Do what you say you’re going to do.** Every time. Especially the small things. If you say you’ll be home at 6, be home at 6. If you say you’ll handle something, handle it. The brain learns safety through pattern recognition, and right now her brain’s pattern detector is probably on high alert for inconsistency.

**Stop defending yourself.** When she’s hurt or scared, your instinct is probably to explain or justify. Don’t. Just listen. “I hear you” works better than “but I didn’t mean it that way.” Her reality is her reality, even if it’s not the one you intended to create.

**Be transparent without being asked.** Volunteer information. Share your phone. Mention who you talked to. Not in a performative “see how honest I am” way, but in a “there are no closed doors between us” way. Opacity breeds suspicion.

**Let her be angry/scared/hurt without trying to fix it immediately.** You can’t logic someone back into feeling safe. She needs to metabolize whatever happened. Your job is to be steady while she does that, not to rush her through it.

**Ask her what she needs.** Specifically. Not “what can I do” but “what would help you feel safer right now?” Then do that thing, even if it feels unfair or uncomfortable.

**Own your part completely.** Whatever broke trust — even if there were reasons, context, mitigating factors — take full responsibility for your side first. No “yes but,” no “you also,” not yet. Maybe not ever, depending on what happened.
